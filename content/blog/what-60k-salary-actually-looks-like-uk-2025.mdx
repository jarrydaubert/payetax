For 2026/27, PayeTax estimates that a £60,000 annual salary produces take-home pay of about **£45,365 a year**, or **£3,780 a month**.

That is a modelled PAYE result, not a statement of exactly what every employee will receive.

## Assumptions

The estimate uses:

- England, Wales or Northern Ireland Income Tax
- tax code [1257L](https://www.gov.uk/tax-codes/what-your-tax-code-means) for England and Northern Ireland or `C1257L` for Wales
- 12 equal monthly pay periods
- a Month 1-style steady-pay projection repeated across those 12 periods, not a cumulative month-by-month payroll simulation
- employee National Insurance category A
- no pension contribution
- no Student Loan
- no benefits, other income or other payroll deductions

## £60,000 PAYE Estimate

| Basis | Gross pay | Income Tax | Employee NI | Estimated take-home |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 12 equal monthly periods | £60,000 | £11,425 | £3,210 | **£45,365** |
| One monthly period | £5,000 | £952 | £268 | **£3,780** |

The annual row is twelve modelled monthly payroll results added together. Actual deductions can differ because of tax-code basis, cumulative pay, irregular earnings, benefits or other payroll adjustments.

## Why a Simplified Annual Calculation Differs

Applying the published annual thresholds directly gives a slightly different illustration:

The Income Tax side illustrates annual liability under the stated assumptions. The NI side applies annualised thresholds only for comparison; ordinary employee NI is assessed by earnings period.

| Method | Income Tax | Employee NI | Take-home |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Monthly payroll model × 12 | £11,424.72 | £3,210.00 | £45,365.28 |
| Simplified annual illustration | £11,432.00 | £3,210.60 | £45,357.40 |

The annual Income Tax illustration uses the £12,570 Personal Allowance, charges 20% on £37,700 and 40% on the remaining £9,730. The annual NI illustration charges 8% through the Upper Earnings Limit and 2% above it.

PAYE and employee NI are operated by pay period. PayeTax therefore uses HMRC's monthly free-pay, band and NI thresholds, together with payroll rounding, for the headline estimate. The simplified annual calculation is useful for explaining the bands, but it is not a monthly payslip calculation.

## What Higher-Rate Tax Means at £60,000

With the standard Personal Allowance, the [higher-rate boundary](https://www.gov.uk/income-tax-rates) is £50,270 of total income. Only the slice above that boundary is taxed at 40% in this illustration.

For a category A employee, that same slice is generally above the NI Upper Earnings Limit and attracts 2% employee NI. The combined marginal deduction on that slice is therefore 42%, but 42% is not the effective rate on the whole £60,000.

## With a Plan 2 Student Loan

The [Plan 2 thresholds for 2026/27](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/special-rules-for-student-loans) are £29,385 annually, £2,448.75 monthly and £565.09 weekly, with deductions at 9% above the threshold for the pay period.

Using HMRC's [monthly deduction rules](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sl3-student-loan-deduction-tables/2026-to-2027-student-and-postgraduate-loan-deduction-tables) for the same £60,000 example:

- monthly Plan 2 deduction: £229
- annual Plan 2 deduction across 12 equal monthly pay periods: £2,748
- estimated annual take-home after tax, NI and Plan 2: £42,617
- estimated monthly take-home: £3,551

Payroll rounds each Student Loan deduction down to a whole pound. Irregular pay or a different pay frequency can therefore change the annual total.

## With a 5% Salary Sacrifice

If 5% of the whole £60,000 salary is validly sacrificed in exchange for an employer pension contribution, with the other base assumptions unchanged including no Student Loan, PayeTax models:

- £3,000 contributed by the employer
- £57,000 remaining contractual cash pay
- estimated annual take-home: £43,625
- estimated monthly take-home: £3,635

This example is only for [salary sacrifice](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/salary-sacrifice-and-the-effects-on-paye). It is not a generic "5% workplace pension" result.

With [net pay](https://www.gov.uk/workplace-pensions/managing-your-pension), the employee contribution is deducted before Income Tax but does not reduce National Insurance. With relief at source, the contribution is taken after tax and NI, the provider adds basic-rate relief, and a higher-rate taxpayer may need to claim additional relief. A scheme may also calculate contributions from qualifying earnings rather than the whole salary.

This is why a categorical statement such as "£1,000 in a pension costs £600" is not a reliable payslip estimate without defining the contribution method, gross versus net amount, tax position and how any further relief is claimed.

## If You Are a Scottish Taxpayer

Scottish taxpayers use different [Income Tax rates and bands](https://www.gov.uk/scottish-income-tax). Scottish taxpayer status normally depends on where you live, not where your employer is based.

Use the [Scottish tax calculator](/tools/scottish-tax-calculator) for a current comparison. Employee NI and Student Loan rules are separate from the Scottish Income Tax bands.

## What This Salary Figure Cannot Establish

A gross salary alone does not establish an income percentile, social class, housing budget, mortgage offer, appropriate savings target or likely lifestyle. Those conclusions need a defined dataset or the person's household circumstances.

Material differences in take-home can also come from tax code, pay pattern, pension method, Student Loan plan, taxable benefits, other income, NI category and Scottish taxpayer status.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much is £60,000 after tax in 2026/27?

Under the assumptions in this article, PayeTax estimates about £45,365 annual take-home, or £3,780 a month, after Income Tax and employee National Insurance.

### Why does the annual illustration give a different result?

The headline estimate adds twelve monthly payroll calculations, including period thresholds and rounding. The separate £45,357.40 annual illustration applies annual thresholds directly, so it should not be divided by 12 and treated as a payslip result.

### Is all of a £60,000 salary taxed at 40%?

No. Under the assumptions here, the Personal Allowance and basic-rate band are used first. Only the £9,730 slice above the £50,270 higher-rate boundary is taxed at 40% in the simplified annual illustration.

### What is the Plan 2 take-home estimate at £60,000?

With the same assumptions plus a Plan 2 loan, the estimate is about £42,617 a year or £3,551 a month. Irregular pay can change deductions because payroll uses period thresholds and rounds each deduction down to a whole pound.

### Does a 5% pension always produce the figure shown here?

No. The figure shown is for 5% of the whole salary under salary sacrifice. Net pay, relief at source and contributions based on qualifying earnings produce different payslip results.
