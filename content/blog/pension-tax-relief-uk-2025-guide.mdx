Pension tax relief means money you'd otherwise pay in Income Tax goes into your pension instead. In 2025-26, every £100 that lands in your pension costs a basic rate taxpayer £80. Higher and additional rate taxpayers can bring that down to £60 and £55, though depending on their scheme they may need to claim part of it themselves.

This guide explains how relief reaches your pension, when you need to claim it, and the limits on how much you can pay in each year. Scottish rates differ, and we cover those separately.

## At a Glance

- **Two ways to get relief**: Relief at source schemes add 20% and you claim any extra; net pay schemes take your contribution before tax, so full relief is automatic
- **Higher and additional rate**: In a relief at source scheme, you claim the extra 20% or 25% through Self Assessment or from HMRC
- **Tax relief limit**: Relief on your own contributions is capped at 100% of your UK earnings (or £3,600 gross if you earn less)
- **Annual allowance**: Usually £60,000 for total pension savings, including your employer's contributions
- **Carry forward**: Unused allowance from the previous 3 tax years may let you pay in more
- **Salary sacrifice**: If your employer offers it, you can save National Insurance as well as tax


## What is Pension Tax Relief?

Pension tax relief is how the government encourages retirement saving. In effect, the money you pay into a pension comes out of your income before tax rather than after. How that happens depends on your scheme, which we cover in the next section.

### The Numbers in Plain English

| Your Tax Rate | In Your Pension | Tax Relief | Cost to You |
|---------------|-----------------|------------|-------------|
| Basic (20%) | £100 | £20 | £80 |
| Higher (40%) | £100 | £40 | £60 |
| Additional (45%) | £100 | £45 | £55 |

These figures assume the whole £100 falls within that tax band.

**Example**: You want £100 in your pension as a higher rate taxpayer in a relief at source scheme.
- You contribute £80 from your bank account
- Your pension provider claims £20 from HMRC (basic rate relief)
- You claim another £20 via Self Assessment (higher rate relief)
- **Cost to you: £60 for £100 in your pension**


## How Pension Tax Relief Works: The Two Systems

Pension schemes give tax relief in one of two ways. It's worth knowing which yours uses, because it decides whether you need to claim anything.

### 1. Relief at Source

**Used by**: Most personal pensions, SIPPs, stakeholder pensions, and many workplace schemes.

**How it works**:
1. You contribute from your **after-tax** income (money in your bank account)
2. Your pension provider claims **basic rate (20%) relief** from HMRC
3. This happens **automatically**—you don't need to do anything
4. If you're a higher/additional rate taxpayer, you claim the extra yourself

**Example** (Higher rate taxpayer contributing £200/month):
- You set up a £200/month direct debit
- Pension provider adds £50 (20% of the gross £250)
- £250 lands in your pension
- You claim £50 more via Self Assessment
- **Cost to you: £150 for £250 in your pension**

**Important**: The contribution shown on your pension statement is the **gross** amount (£250 in this example), not what you actually paid.

### 2. Net Pay Arrangement

**Used by**: Many workplace pension schemes (especially public sector, large employers).

**How it works**:
1. Your contribution is taken from your salary **before** tax is calculated
2. You automatically get relief at your **full marginal rate**
3. No need to claim anything via Self Assessment
4. Shows as lower taxable income on your payslip

**Example** (Higher rate taxpayer with £500/month contribution):
- Gross salary: £5,000/month
- Pension contribution: £500 (taken before tax)
- Taxable salary: £4,500
- Tax calculated on £4,500 instead of £5,000
- **Full 40% relief applied automatically**

**The upside**: Higher and additional rate taxpayers get full relief straight away, with nothing to claim.

### Which System Does My Pension Use?

**Check your payslip**:
- **Net pay**: Pension contribution shown *before* tax calculations, reduces your taxable pay
- **Relief at source**: Pension contribution shown *after* tax calculations, taxable pay unchanged

**Or check your annual pension statement**:
- **Net pay**: Shows only your contributions
- **Relief at source**: Shows your contributions plus "tax relief claimed"

**Still unsure?** Ask your HR department or pension provider directly.


## Claiming Higher Rate Tax Relief

If you pay higher or additional rate tax and your pension uses **relief at source**, you need to claim the extra relief yourself.

### How to Claim

**Via Self Assessment**:
1. Complete boxes on the tax return for pension contributions
2. Enter the **gross** amount (your contribution + basic rate relief)
3. HMRC calculates the additional relief due
4. You receive a refund or reduced tax bill

**Via HMRC directly** (if you don't file Self Assessment):
1. Call HMRC: 0300 200 3300
2. Or write to: Pay As You Earn and Self Assessment, HM Revenue and Customs, BX9 1AS
3. Provide details of your pension contributions
4. HMRC will adjust your tax code or send a refund

### How Much Can You Claim Back?

| Tax Rate | Basic Relief (Automatic) | You Claim | Total Relief |
|----------|-------------------------|-----------|--------------|
| Basic (20%) | 20% | Nothing | 20% |
| Higher (40%) | 20% | 20% | 40% |
| Additional (45%) | 20% | 25% | 45% |

**Important**: The relief is based on your **marginal rate**—the highest rate you pay. If only part of your income is taxed at 40%, you only get 40% relief on contributions up to that amount.

### The Deadline

You have **4 years** from the end of the tax year to claim higher rate relief, so it's worth checking earlier years too.


## Annual Allowance: How Much Can You Contribute?

Two separate limits apply, and it helps to keep them apart:

1. **Your tax relief limit**: You can get tax relief on your own contributions up to 100% of your relevant UK earnings for the year, such as salary, bonuses and self-employed profits. If you earn less than £3,600, you can still get relief on up to £3,600 gross through a relief at source scheme. Your employer's contributions don't count towards this limit.
2. **The annual allowance**: This caps your *total* pension savings for the year. For defined contribution pensions (most workplace and personal pensions), that means your contributions (including the basic rate relief added to them) plus your employer's. Defined benefit (final salary or career average) schemes instead count how much the value of your promised pension has increased over the year. Going over the allowance can mean an annual allowance tax charge.

### Standard Annual Allowance: £60,000

For the 2025-26 tax year, the standard annual allowance is **£60,000**. It can be lower if the taper applies (see below), or £10,000 for defined contribution savings if you've already started taking money flexibly from a pension (the "money purchase annual allowance").

The two examples below assume defined contribution pensions, the standard £60,000 allowance, no taper or money purchase annual allowance, and no carry forward. Personal contributions are shown gross: in a relief at source scheme, that includes the basic rate relief your provider adds.

**Example**: You earn £45,000 and your employer contributes £3,000.
- Your own contributions can get relief up to: £45,000 gross (100% of earnings)
- If you contribute that, total pension savings: £45,000 + £3,000 = £48,000
- Annual allowance left: £60,000 − £48,000 = £12,000

Your employer's £3,000 doesn't reduce the £45,000 you can get relief on. In a relief at source scheme, £45,000 gross means you pay £36,000 and your provider adds £9,000. The £12,000 left could still be used by extra employer contributions, but you couldn't get tax relief on personal contributions above £45,000 gross.

**Example**: You earn £80,000 and your employer contributes £8,000.
- Your own contributions can get relief up to: £80,000 gross (100% of earnings)
- Annual allowance: £60,000, of which your employer uses £8,000
- Most you can contribute without going over the allowance: £52,000 gross

Here the annual allowance, not your earnings, is the tighter limit—unless you have unused allowance to carry forward.

### Tapered Annual Allowance (High Earners)

For 2025-26, if your "threshold income" exceeds £200,000 and your "adjusted income" exceeds £260,000, your annual allowance is reduced.

**The taper**:
- For every £2 of adjusted income over £260,000, allowance reduces by £1
- Minimum allowance: £10,000 (reached at £360,000 adjusted income)

Threshold income and adjusted income each have their own step-by-step calculation. Use HMRC's guide to [work out your tapered annual allowance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/pension-schemes-work-out-your-tapered-annual-allowance).

If your income is near these levels, it's worth getting professional advice.

### Carry Forward: Use Previous Years' Allowances

Didn't use your full allowance in previous years? You can **carry forward** unused amounts from the last 3 tax years.

**Rules**:
- You must have been a member of a registered pension scheme in those years
- Current year's allowance is used first
- Oldest unused allowance is used next
- Carry forward increases your annual allowance, not the earnings limit on tax relief for your own contributions
- It can't be used to increase the money purchase annual allowance

**Example**: You want to contribute £100,000 gross this year (including any basic rate relief) to a defined contribution pension, with no taper or money purchase annual allowance, and your employer contributes nothing.
- 2025-26 allowance: £60,000
- 2022-23 unused: £5,000
- 2023-24 unused: £15,000
- 2024-25 unused: £20,000
- **Total annual allowance available: £100,000** ✓

To get tax relief on all £100,000 yourself, you'd also need relevant UK earnings of at least £100,000 in 2025-26. If you earn £70,000, relief on your own contributions stops at £70,000 gross; the other £30,000 of allowance would only be useful if your employer also paid in.

Carry forward can help in a year with a large bonus, or if you're catching up on retirement saving. A windfall such as an inheritance can help you pay in more, but tax relief is still limited to your earnings for the year (or £3,600 gross if you earn less).


## Salary Sacrifice: Saving National Insurance Too

If your employer offers **salary sacrifice** for pension contributions, you can save National Insurance as well as Income Tax.

### How Salary Sacrifice Works

1. You agree to reduce your contractual salary
2. Your employer puts the "sacrificed" amount into your pension
3. Because it's now employer contribution, **neither you nor your employer pays National Insurance**

### The Extra Savings

| Your Earnings | Standard Relief | With Salary Sacrifice | Extra Benefit |
|--------------|-----------------|----------------------|---------------|
| £12,571-£50,270 | 20% tax saved | 20% tax + 8% NI | 8% extra |
| £50,271-£125,140 | 40% tax saved | 40% tax + 2% NI | 2% extra |
| Above £125,140 | 45% tax saved | 45% tax + 2% NI | 2% extra |

Your employer also saves 15% employer National Insurance on earnings above £5,000 a year. Some employers pass part of that saving into your pension.

### Example: £500 Pension Contribution

**Without salary sacrifice** (higher rate taxpayer, relief at source):
- You pay £400, and your provider tops it up to £500
- You claim £100 back via Self Assessment
- Cost to you: £300
- You still pay £10 NI (2%) on the £500 of salary

**With salary sacrifice**:
- Your salary is reduced by £500 and your employer pays £500 into your pension
- Your take-home pay falls by £290, because that £500 would have lost £200 in tax and £10 in NI
- Cost to you: £290, which is £10 less than above
- Your employer saves £75 (15%) in employer NI
- **If your employer passes all of that on: £575 in your pension for £290**

### Salary Sacrifice Near £100,000

Salary sacrifice can be especially useful if your income is just over £100,000. By reducing your salary, you can:

1. **Bring your income back towards £100,000** and keep more of your Personal Allowance
2. **Avoid the 60% effective tax rate** on income between £100,000 and £125,140
3. **Save NI** on top of the tax savings

Other pension contributions reduce the income used for this test too, but salary sacrifice also saves NI. Our [guide to the 60% tax rate between £100,000 and £125,140](/blog/100k-tax-trap-avoid-60-percent-tax-2025) explains how it works.


## Scottish Taxpayers: Special Considerations

If you're a Scottish taxpayer, pension tax relief works the same way, but the rates are different.

### Scottish Income Tax Rates 2025-26

| Band | Rate | Income Range |
|------|------|--------------|
| Starter | 19% | £12,571 - £15,397 |
| Basic | 20% | £15,398 - £27,491 |
| Intermediate | 21% | £27,492 - £43,662 |
| Higher | 42% | £43,663 - £75,000 |
| Advanced | 45% | £75,001 - £125,140 |
| Top | 48% | Over £125,140 |

### Relief at Source in Scotland

Relief at source schemes **always add 20%**, even if your highest rate is the 19% starter rate. If that applies to you, you keep the extra 1%.

If you pay 21%, 42%, 45% or 48%, you claim the difference above 20% through Self Assessment or by contacting HMRC, as elsewhere in the UK.


## Common Pension Tax Relief Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Not Claiming Higher Rate Relief

**The problem**: In a relief at source scheme, higher and additional rate relief isn't added automatically. If you don't claim it, you don't get it.

**The fix**:
- If you use Self Assessment, ensure you complete the pension contribution boxes
- If not, contact HMRC to claim or adjust your tax code
- Check the last 4 tax years for missed claims

### Mistake 2: Exceeding Annual Allowance

**The problem**: Total pension savings above your annual allowance (usually £60,000, including your employer's contributions) can trigger a tax charge. Separately, your own contributions above your earnings (or £3,600 gross if you earn less) don't get tax relief.

**The fix**:
- Track all contributions including employer's
- Remember carry forward rules
- Consider spreading large contributions over multiple years

### Mistake 3: Forgetting About Other Pensions

**The problem**: The annual allowance covers all your pensions combined, not each one separately.

**The fix**: Add up what goes into every scheme you or your employers pay into. If you've lost track of an old pension, the [Pension Tracing Service](https://www.gov.uk/find-pension-contact-details) can help you find it.

### Mistake 4: Contributing Without Earnings

**The problem**: You can only get tax relief up to 100% of your UK earnings.

**The fix**: If you have no earnings, you can still contribute £3,600 gross (£2,880 net) per year and receive basic rate relief.


## Pension Tax Relief Calculator

For a rough figure, use your gross contribution and your highest tax rate:

**Your tax relief** = Gross contribution × Your highest tax rate

**Your cost** = Gross contribution × (1 − Your highest tax rate)

**Example**: £10,000 gross contribution as a 40% taxpayer, all within the higher rate band
- Tax relief: £10,000 × 40% = £4,000
- Cost to you: £10,000 × 60% = £6,000

## Pension Contributions and Tax Code Adjustments

If you're a higher rate taxpayer making regular pension contributions via relief at source, HMRC can adjust your tax code to give relief throughout the year (instead of waiting for Self Assessment).

### How It Works

1. Contact HMRC with details of your regular pension contributions
2. They adjust your tax code to increase your tax-free amount
3. You pay less tax each month
4. You don't have to wait for a refund

### Example

You pay £400 a month into a relief at source pension. Your provider adds £100 basic rate relief, so £500 a month (£6,000 a year) goes in gross. Assuming at least £6,000 of your income is taxed at 40%:
- Basic rate relief: £1,200 a year (20% of £6,000), added by your provider automatically
- Extra higher rate relief: £1,200 a year (another 20% of £6,000), normally claimed through Self Assessment or by contacting HMRC

HMRC can instead give you the extra £1,200 through your PAYE tax code, so less tax is taken from your pay during the year rather than refunded later. The exact change to your code depends on your circumstances and when HMRC learns about your contributions, so check the coding notice HMRC sends you.

**Same total relief, better cash flow.**


## Pension Tax Relief vs ISA: Which is Better?

Both pensions and ISAs are tax-efficient, but they work differently.

### Pension Advantages
- ✅ Tax relief on contributions at your Income Tax rate
- ✅ Employer contributions, and salary sacrifice where offered
- ✅ Usually 25% tax-free when you take your pension, within an overall cap

### Pension Disadvantages
- ❌ Usually locked until age 55 (57 from April 2028)
- ❌ Income taxed when withdrawn, apart from the tax-free part
- ❌ Annual allowance and other limits apply

### ISA Advantages
- ✅ Tax-free withdrawals
- ✅ Access at any time
- ✅ Simpler rules

### ISA Disadvantages
- ❌ No upfront tax relief
- ❌ £20,000 annual limit
- ❌ No employer contributions

### The Verdict

**For most people**: Make sure you get your full employer pension match first, as that's money you'd otherwise miss. After that, it depends on when you'll need the money.

**As a rough guide**: The upfront relief makes pensions especially attractive for higher rate taxpayers. If you're a basic rate taxpayer and may need the money before retirement, an ISA's flexibility can matter more.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much tax relief do I get on pension contributions?

You get relief at your highest Income Tax rate: 20% for basic rate, 40% for higher rate and 45% for additional rate. Scottish taxpayers get relief at their Scottish rate, from 19% to 48%, although relief at source schemes always add at least 20%.

### Do I need to claim pension tax relief?

If your pension uses **net pay arrangement**, relief is automatic. If it uses **relief at source**, basic rate (20%) is automatic, but higher/additional rate taxpayers must claim the extra via Self Assessment or directly from HMRC.

### What is the maximum I can contribute to a pension?

There are two limits. You can get tax relief on your own contributions up to 100% of your UK earnings (or £3,600 gross if you earn less). Separately, total contributions from you and your employer above the annual allowance—usually £60,000 for 2025-26—can trigger a tax charge, although you may be able to carry forward unused allowance from the previous 3 years.

### Can I get pension tax relief if I don't work?

Yes, but it's limited. Non-earners can contribute up to £3,600 gross (£2,880 net) per year and still receive basic rate relief.

### Is salary sacrifice better than pension contributions?

Usually yes, because you save National Insurance (8% or 2%) on top of income tax relief. However, it reduces your official salary, which could affect mortgages or benefits.

### How do I claim higher rate pension tax relief?

Through your Self Assessment tax return (enter your gross pension contributions), or, if you don't file a return, by contacting HMRC. HMRC may give the relief through your tax code or as a refund.
