# TaxInsights Voice & Style

Purpose: give writers and reviewers a repeatable, human editorial voice without weakening PayeTax's statutory or calculation standards. Read alongside [BLOG_GUIDE.md](BLOG_GUIDE.md) and [CONTENT_PHILOSOPHY.md](CONTENT_PHILOSOPHY.md). These examples describe style, **not approved tax figures or reusable worked calculations**.

## The reader and promise

We write for UK working adults who have a real question about pay, tax or money but no reason to know payroll jargon. That includes first jobs, pay rises, student loans, pensions, families, side income and directors. Target the **question and life situation**, not a generation, a level of earnings or a supposed lack of intelligence.

**Editorial promise:** Your pay. Your money. Explained.

**House principle:** Write as someone who understands tax well enough to explain it simply. Give readers the answer, explain why it is true, and show what it means for their money.

## The voice

- **Direct and answer-first:** Lead with the useful answer or the reader's recognisable situation. A short opening should establish the result, the consequence and what comes next. Do not label it “TL;DR” by default.
- **Human, not performative:** Address the reader as “you” where natural, use ordinary UK contractions, and vary sentence rhythm. Neither “you” frequency nor sentence length is a quota.
- **Confident without hype:** Show the working; avoid “shocking truth”, “secret trick”, fear, promises of effortless savings and implied HMRC endorsement.
- **Warm but restrained:** A dry aside can help when genuine. No required joke per section, memes, forced slang or condescension.
- **Concrete:** Use familiar pay periods, real-world consequences and a specific worked case when one helps. Named fictional people are optional; do not invent quotes or stories presented as real.
- **Clear about uncertainty:** State the tax year, location, eligibility and legal status where they matter. Do not blur a proposal into enacted law or an illustrative result into a universal payslip.
- **Useful from beginning to end:** Every section must answer a new reader question, add an example, resolve a misconception or explain an action. Avoid repeating the opening in different words.

Read a draft aloud. If it sounds like a compliance circular, make it more natural. If it sounds like a sales pitch, remove the hype. Accuracy survives both edits.

## Opening: answer and consequence

Open in roughly two or three clear sentences where practical; length follows the subject. A salary result normally leads with the number. A myth-buster can lead with the misconception and then correct it. A Budget update identifies who is affected before legal detail.

- Avoid: “In this article, we will explore the various factors influencing take-home remuneration.”
- Prefer: “Your salary isn't what lands in your bank account. Income Tax, National Insurance and sometimes a pension or student loan come off first. Here's how to check the difference.”

No standalone “Quick Summary” is required if the opening already does the job. A table or short summary remains welcome where it adds something, such as a Budget with several affected groups.

## Five flexible formats

Pick **one primary format**; combine elements only when the reader benefits. These are planning shapes, not a content generator or mandatory headings.

| Format | Reader comes asking | Useful shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salary snapshot | “What is £X after tax?” | Net result → one readable payslip breakdown → assumptions → what changes the number → try your own salary |
| Explainer | “How does this work?” | Familiar situation → concept explained in plain English → example or reframe → what to check |
| Problem-solver | “Why did my pay change?” | Symptom and answer → likely causes → how to check each → what action to take |
| What's changed | “Does this Budget/rule affect me?” | Who is affected → what it means → effective dates and legal status → scenario → action |
| Myth-buster / decision | “Is this tax belief right?” | Belief → correction → evidence and a worked consequence → when the answer differs |

Article length follows complexity. A brief salary answer can be a few hundred words; a complicated guide may need several thousand. No minimum, maximum or automatic FAQ. Headings can be questions **or** clear statements. Keep tables accessible and short enough to use on mobile; do not force tables because a format suggests one.

## Caveats and citations: necessary, not defensive

**Keep a qualification beside its claim whenever omitting it could materially mislead the reader or change their decision.** Examples: Scottish rates versus rest-of-UK rates, pension method, tax code, student-loan plan, qualifying criteria, legal status, effective date and whether the calculator supports the scenario. Move secondary model assumptions into one concise “What this assumes” section where suitable. Retain the appropriate closing disclaimer; do not repeat the same caveat after every paragraph.

Explain the practical effect first, but do not move a crucial exclusion so far away that a reader could act on an incorrect impression. Cite official sources close to contested or changeable claims or with clear references readers can follow; a final Sources section can supplement, not hide, provenance. No detached unsourced numbers.

Tax facts and legal claims follow BLOG_GUIDE's source hierarchy. For every supported-year worked calculation, record the exact engine inputs and outputs in the PR **and** check against independent statutory fixtures or hand-derived evidence. The engine cannot certify itself. Future-year proposals are explained, never presented as calculator results. Current pension modelling assumes salary sacrifice unless a later verified implementation changes that contract.

## Before and after: examples from our own writing

**Salary guide**

Before: “That is a modelled PAYE result, not a statement of exactly what every employee will receive.”

Better pattern: “On a £X salary, you'd take home about £Y a month, using a standard tax code and no pension or student loan deductions. Here's where the rest goes.”

Why: lead with the answer and put the assumptions in the sentence that explains it. **£X/£Y are placeholders, not publishable figures.**

**National Insurance**

Before: “National Insurance is separate from Income Tax, but for employees it feels similar because it comes out of pay through PAYE.”

Better: “Income Tax and National Insurance sit next to each other on your payslip. They follow different rules, but both reduce what reaches your account.”

Why: start with something readers recognise; explain the distinctions in the next section.

**Budget update**

Before: “Budget 2026, delivered on 28 October 2026, proposes higher tax on savings and property income…”

Better pattern: “What does the Budget change for your payslip? Start with the main salary rules, then look at what's changing for savings, rental income and other situations. Separate decisions already in law from proposals that still need legislation.”

Why: lead with the reader's priority and preserve legal-status distinctions. **This is a structural example, not a substitute for dated Budget facts.**

**Technical rule**

Before: “The application of the Personal Allowance is subject to an income-based taper for individuals…”

Better: “Above the relevant income threshold, you start losing some of your tax-free allowance. That can make the next pound of a pay rise cost more in tax than you'd expect.”

Why: explain cause and effect before introducing a technical name. Insert exact thresholds only after current verification.

## Reader trust and distinctiveness

Use PayeTax's strengths: specific, reproducible payroll examples, independent cross-checks, useful links to the **existing** calculator, and honest modelling limits. Never pretend that general spending illustrations or a hypothetical person's story came from real readers. Competitor examples are for studying structure and reader needs, not for copying prose or claiming their techniques caused rankings.

Do not imply an editorial staff or a regulated tax-advice service. Keep existing article bylines until a separate, explicitly reviewed attribution decision. Use “we” for PayeTax only where it describes actual work or tool behaviour.

## Review in one pass, two verdicts

The existing independent review must assess **both**:

1. **Accuracy:** tax year, region, rules, evidence, independently checked numbers, eligibility, legal status, scope and load-bearing caveats.
2. **Readability:** answer-first opening, natural voice, reader-specific usefulness beyond the intro, varied rhythm, unnecessary repetition, useful headings/examples, accessible mobile presentation and non-promotional links.

Reviewers may **remove** redundant caveats and generic filler, while preserving anything material. Neither verdict passes merely because CI/build passed. The maintainer spot-checks the opening and a representative middle section on a real rendered page before authorising merge.

## Adopting the standard

Pilot on a small, deliberately chosen set of articles before any library-wide rewrite. The Budget article's post-announcement traffic is volatile, and established salary pages with documented organic visibility require search-evidence protection. Do not require a named character, fixed word count, joke, FAQ, table or new tracking instrumentation as an editorial gate. Preserve the existing repo's opt-in analytics/privacy rules.
