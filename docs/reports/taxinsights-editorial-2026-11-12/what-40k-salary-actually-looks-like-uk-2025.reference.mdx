On a £40,000 salary you take home about **£32,322 a year**, or **£2,694 a month**, in 2026/27.

Income Tax takes £457 a month and National Insurance £183. Between them, about 19p of every pound you earn is gone before it reaches your account.

That is the simplest version of £40,000: you live in England, Wales or Northern Ireland, you're on the standard tax code, and nothing else comes out of your pay. If you pay into a pension or have a student loan, your number will be lower, and the sections below show by how much. Treat all of it as a close estimate, not a copy of your payslip. [What this assumes](#what-this-assumes) lists everything behind the figures.

## Where the money goes

| Basis | Gross pay | Income Tax | Employee NI | Estimated take-home |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 12 equal monthly periods | £40,000 | £5,484 | £2,194 | **£32,322** |
| One monthly period | £3,333 | £457 | £183 | **£2,694** |

Amounts are rounded to the nearest pound, so a row can be £1 out if you add it up yourself.

In this example, Income Tax and National Insurance are worked out on different clocks. Income Tax uses a yearly allowance: your tax code spreads the standard £12,570 Personal Allowance across the year, and you pay 20% on your pay above it. National Insurance has no yearly allowance. Each payslip is assessed on its own, at 8% of that month's pay above £1,048, which comes to £183 a month here. At £40,000 all of your taxable pay sits in the basic-rate band, so none of it is taxed at 40%.

That also gives a rough guide to a pay rise. On the same assumptions, and while your salary stays below £50,270, you keep about 72p of each extra pound, because roughly 20p goes in Income Tax and 8p in National Insurance. A pension contribution or student loan changes that figure, and payroll rounding means it will not be exact on any one payslip.

## If you have a student loan

A Plan 2 loan takes 9% of what you earn above £29,385 a year, which payroll applies as £2,448.75 a month. On £40,000 that is £79 a month, or £948 a year, and it brings your take-home down to about **£2,615 a month** (£31,374 a year).

Payroll applies the threshold to each pay period and [rounds each deduction down to a whole pound](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sl3-student-loan-deduction-tables/2026-to-2027-student-and-postgraduate-loan-deduction-tables), so a bonus month, uneven pay or a different pay frequency changes the yearly total.

Other plans start at different thresholds, and the [Plan 2 figures](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/special-rules-for-student-loans) apply only if that is your plan. If you aren't sure which one you have, our [student loan guide](/blog/student-loan-repayment-changes-2025-26) shows how to tell.

## If you pay into a pension

Say you put 5% of your salary into your pension through salary sacrifice. Your employer pays £2,000 a year into the pension, your contractual pay becomes £38,000, and your take-home falls to about **£2,574 a month** (£30,883 a year).

Look at that against the first figure. £167 a month goes into your pension, but your take-home drops by only £120. The other £47 is Income Tax and National Insurance you no longer pay.

How you pay in matters, because the three common methods produce three different payslips:

- That figure is only for [salary sacrifice](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/salary-sacrifice-and-the-effects-on-paye), where you agree to a lower salary and your employer pays the difference into your pension. It assumes 5% of your whole salary. It is not a general "5% workplace pension" result.
- With [net pay](https://www.gov.uk/workplace-pensions/managing-your-pension), your contribution is taken before Income Tax, but you still pay National Insurance on it.
- With relief at source, your contribution is taken after tax and National Insurance, and your pension provider adds basic-rate tax relief. The amount on your payslip is therefore smaller than the amount that reaches your pension.

Your scheme may also work out the percentage on qualifying earnings instead of your whole salary. Your employer or pension provider can tell you which method and which basis apply to you.

## How £40,000 compares

The [ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings release of 22 October 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/earningsandworkinghours/bulletins/annualsurveyofhoursandearnings/2026) reported median gross annual earnings of £40,911 in April 2026 for full-time employees who had been in their jobs for at least a year. So £40,000 is £911 below the median for that group.

The figure is provisional and describes that group only. It can't tell you where £40,000 sits among all workers, or how far it goes with your rent, mortgage or household.

## If you live in Scotland

Scotland sets its own [Income Tax rates and bands](https://www.gov.uk/scottish-income-tax), so the Income Tax above won't match yours. What counts is normally where you live, not where your employer is based. National Insurance and student loan deductions follow their own rules and don't change with the Scottish bands.

The [Scottish tax calculator](/tools/scottish-tax-calculator) gives you the comparison for your salary.

## What this assumes

The headline figure uses:

- England, Wales or Northern Ireland Income Tax
- tax code [1257L](https://www.gov.uk/tax-codes/what-your-tax-code-means) for England and Northern Ireland or `C1257L` for Wales
- 12 equal monthly pay periods
- a Month 1-style steady-pay projection repeated across those 12 periods, not a cumulative month-by-month payroll simulation
- employee National Insurance category A
- no pension contribution
- no Student Loan
- no benefits, other income or other payroll deductions

The student loan and pension examples each change one of those and keep the rest.

Your own payslip will differ if any of them isn't true for you: for example, if you get bonuses or overtime, your tax code is different or runs on a Week 1 or Month 1 basis, you have benefits, taxable expenses or other income, or you are in a different National Insurance category or have reached State Pension age.

## How the figures are worked out

PAYE and National Insurance are calculated each pay period, not once a year. So the headline figure runs one month of pay through HMRC's monthly free-pay, tax-band and National Insurance thresholds, with payroll rounding, and multiplies the result by twelve.

Do the sum on annual figures instead and you land £2.44 lower:

| Method | Income Tax | Employee NI | Take-home |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Monthly payroll model × 12 | £5,484.00 | £2,193.96 | £32,322.04 |
| Simplified annual illustration | £5,486.00 | £2,194.40 | £32,319.60 |

The annual version subtracts the £12,570 Personal Allowance, charges 20% on the remaining £27,430, and applies 8% National Insurance above the £12,570 Primary Threshold. It is the easier way to see how the bands work, but it is not how a payslip is calculated. Employee National Insurance in particular is assessed pay period by pay period, so the annual figure is there only for comparison. Don't divide it by twelve and expect it to match your pay.
